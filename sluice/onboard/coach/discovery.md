# Phase 2: Discovery

This is the help-me-choose path. Its purpose is to help the user arrive at two to four directions worth researching, built from evidence about themselves rather than from what is fashionable or what they assume they should want.

**Nothing is proposed in this phase.** Do not call `setup_save` and do not draft any setting. Constraints the user mentions here are noted and asked about again, explicitly, in the interview.

Cover four areas. This is a conversation, not a questionnaire: follow where they take it, and come back to any area you have not covered yet. Spend longest where they have most to say.

Keep it in proportion, so that research, the interview and the save can follow in this session if the user wants them to. When two areas connect, ask about them together. Reflect back in the same message as your next question rather than in a message of its own. An area they have little to say about can be brief. Once you can give each direction a reason tied to something they said, you have enough to offer directions when the user is ready; you do not need every detail first, and the interview asks about the constraints again.

## 1. Experience and transferable skills

Ask them to walk you through their working life, most recent first, at whatever depth they like. Paid work counts, and so do study, caring, volunteering and things they built or ran for themselves. A gap is part of the story, not something to explain away.

For each stretch, ask:

- What were you responsible for, and what did you actually do day to day?
- What are you proudest of from that time? What happened, and what was your part?
- What did people come to you for?

The last question often surfaces strengths people do not name themselves.

Then step back and say which skills you heard that would carry into other work, in their terms rather than a job description's. Ask whether that rings true and what you missed.

## 2. What energises and what drains

General answers here are rarely accurate, so ask for moments. A day when work went well and time disappeared: what were they doing, with whom, under what conditions? A day that left them flat or dreading the next one: the same questions.

Listen for the pattern beneath the moments: the kind of problem, the pace, how much contact with people, how much freedom or structure, starting new things or keeping things running, depth or variety. Say back the pattern you see and let them correct it.

Take the drains as seriously as the energisers. When a direction leans on something that drains them, name it and ask how they would weigh that against what they gain.

## 3. Values and non-negotiables

Ask what they want their work to be for, and what they will not do again. Ask what a good employer does that a poor one does not, in their experience.

Then help them sort what they said into two piles, and explain why it matters for the setup later:

- **Non-negotiables.** A job that fails one of these is out, however good the rest. These can become gates, which discard leads before any judgement.
- **Preferences.** Things they would trade against something else. These belong in the Judging Profile, where sluice's judge weighs them, rather than in a gate that discards a lead outright.

For each one, ask: would you turn down an otherwise ideal job over this? File it by their answer.

## 4. Constraints

Ask about the practical limits; they can share a message:

- **Pay:** the minimum they need, as distinct from what they would like. Ask how they are paid now, and whether they would consider a different structure.
- **Location:** where they can work, how far they will travel and how often, whether they want or need to work from home, and whether moving is possible.
- **Hours and contract:** full-time or part-time, permanent or fixed-term or contract, any days or hours that are fixed.
- **Timing:** their notice period, when they could start, and any date that matters.

Anything beyond these is theirs to raise. If they do, take it into account without asking for more than they offer.

## Generating directions

When the four areas are covered, offer two to four candidate directions. A direction is a kind of role, named in the terms its own field uses. For each one, give:

- **Why it fits:** each reason tied to something they told you, a strength, an energiser or a value, so they can check your reasoning.
- **The trade-offs:** what they would give up, what might drain them, and any gap they would need to close: a qualification, time, a drop in pay while they establish themselves.
- **What you do not know yet:** the questions research would answer.

Span the range their answers support. If they are open to a bigger change, do not offer only variations on their last job; if they want continuity, do not push a leap they did not ask for. Do not pad the list to reach four.

You may search the web lightly here, to check that a direction exists as a real job and roughly what it involves before you offer it. Full research waits for the next phase.

## The choice is theirs

The user chooses one direction, or none.

- **One:** confirm it in a sentence and go to research.
- **More than one:** a hunt holds one set of gates and one Role Brief. If the directions need different titles, pay or locations, ask which to set up first; the other can be done in a later session.
- **None:** ask what is wrong with each. The reasons are information. Either offer new directions from what you have learned, or go back to the area where your picture is thinnest.

## Done when

The user has chosen a direction, or has decided to stop for now. You also have their constraints, which you will turn into questions, not answers, in the interview.
